Some of the world's best-known brands started as words that did not exist. Invented, or "brandable", names are short, distinctive and much easier to secure as a domain and trademark than ordinary words. They are also easy to get wrong: a made-up word can just as easily sound like a medicine, a typo or something rude in another language. This guide explains the techniques behind good brandable names and how to judge the ones our generator creates for you.

Why choose a brandable name?

  • Availability: almost every common English word is already a .com. Invented words often are not.
  • Protection: trademark offices favour distinctive names. Invented words are the most distinctive of all, so they are usually easier to register and defend than descriptive names like "Quality Plumbing".
  • Flexibility: a name that does not describe a product never goes out of date when you change what you sell.
  • Memorability: once people know it, a short unusual word stands out in a list of generic competitors.

The trade-off is meaning. A brandable name tells customers nothing on its own, so you need a clear tagline and more marketing effort early on. For a local trade that relies on people instantly understanding what you do, a distinctive-word-plus-descriptor name (for example "Kavo Plumbing") can give you the best of both.

Techniques for creating brandable names

Blends (portmanteaus)

Blend two meaningful words that share letters: kawaii + ink = Kawaiink, pipe + perfect = Piperfect. The overlap makes the join feel natural. Good blends keep both source words recognisable; bad ones leave people unsure what they are looking at.

Clipping and suffixes

Take the first syllable of a meaningful word and add a friendly ending: flow → Flowly, code → Codester, pixel → Pixora. Common endings include -ly, -io, -ify, -ora, -ex, -ix, -o and -a. Each carries a feel: -ly and -ify sound like modern apps, -ora and -a sound soft and premium, -ex and -ix sound technical.

Compounds

Join two short real words into one: Pipeworks, Glowhub, Leafwise. Compounds are easier to understand than pure inventions and often still available as domains.

Pure inventions

Build a word from pleasant syllables: Kavora, Lumio, Tavio. Consonant-vowel patterns (CV-CV or CV-CV-CV) are easiest to say in most languages. Our generator's "Short & brandable" style creates these by starting with sounds from your keywords so the result still feels connected to your business.

Borrowed words

Words from other languages (including Irish) can work as brandable names: Solas, Grian, Réalta. They have real meaning and a story to tell, which helps with marketing. Always check the meaning with a native speaker.

Sound symbolism: why some made-up words feel right

Research in linguistics has shown that people associate certain sounds with certain qualities, even in words they have never heard. You can use this deliberately:

  • Soft sounds (m, l, n, w) and rounded vowels (o, oo): feel gentle, warm, comforting. Good for bakeries, baby products, wellness and beauty.
  • Hard sounds (k, t, p, x, z) and short vowels (i, e): feel quick, precise, energetic. Good for tech, fitness and trades.
  • Front vowels (ee, i): suggest small, light and fast.
  • Back vowels (o, u, ah): suggest big, solid and strong.

So "Lumo" feels like a calm skincare brand, and "Kitex" feels like a software tool, before anyone tells you what they do.

How to judge a brandable name

Run every invented name through these tests before it makes your shortlist:

  1. Say it: is there only one obvious way to pronounce it? If people could say it two ways, they will.
  2. Spell it: say it to someone and ask them to type it. Ideally they get it right first time.
  3. Check its meaning: search it in Google, Google Translate and Urban Dictionary. Invented words sometimes mean something unfortunate in another language or in slang.
  4. Check its neighbours: does it sound too close to a famous brand? That is a trademark risk and makes you look like a copy.
  5. Check the domain and handles: the whole point of a brandable name is availability, so if the .com and Instagram are taken, move on.
  6. Picture it on your product: on the van, the cup, the app icon, the invoice. Does it look like a real business?

Common brandable name mistakes

  • Too many letters: brandable names work best at 4-8 letters. Longer invented words are hard to remember.
  • Random letter combinations: names like "Xqyra" look clever but nobody can say them.
  • Sounding like a pharmaceutical: lots of -ex, -ix and -ia endings can make a name sound like a prescription. Fine for tech, odd for a cake shop.
  • Copying the trend of the year: every era has its fashionable ending. Pick something that still sounds good once the trend passes.
  • No supporting tagline: pair a brandable name with a short explanation everywhere it appears: "Kavora: handmade kawaii stationery".
