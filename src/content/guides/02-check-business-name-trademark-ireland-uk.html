--- title: How to Check if a Business Name Is Trademarked in Ireland and the UK short: Checking trademarks (Ireland & UK) description: Step-by-step guide to checking a business name against the CRO, Companies House, IPOI, UK IPO and EUIPO registers, plus what the results mean and when to get advice. date: 2026-11-16 ---

Finding a name you love is exciting. Discovering six months later that someone else owns the trademark is not. Rebranding a small business, with new signage, packaging, a new website and lost search rankings, can easily cost more than the business made in its first year. The good news is that the basic checks are free, can be done online in an evening, and catch the vast majority of problems.

This guide explains which registers to search if you trade in Ireland or the UK, how to read the results, and when it is worth paying a professional. It is general information, not legal advice; if you find a close match, speak to a trade mark attorney or solicitor.

Business names, company names and trademarks are different things

People often assume that registering a name with the CRO or Companies House means they "own" it. It does not. There are three separate layers:

  • Business (trading) name: in Ireland, any name you trade under that is not your own true name must be registered with the Companies Registration Office (CRO). This is a transparency register so the public can see who is behind a business. The CRO itself states that registration does not give you exclusive rights to the name. In the UK, sole traders and partnerships do not register trading names at all.
  • Company name: a limited company's registered name. The CRO and Companies House will refuse names that are the same as (or, in some cases, too like) an existing company name, but they do not check trademarks.
  • Trademark: a registered right to stop others using the same or a confusingly similar name for the same or similar goods or services. This is the layer that actually protects your brand, and the one most likely to cause trouble if you clash with it.

On top of these, both Irish and UK law recognise passing off: if another business has built up a reputation under a name, even without registering a trademark, it may be able to stop you using something confusingly similar. That is why a simple Google search is part of the process too.

Step 1: Search the company registers

Ireland: the CRO's online system, CORE, lets you search both the companies register and the business names register free of charge. Search your exact name, then variations: with and without "the", singular and plural, and with common misspellings.

UK: use the Companies House "Find and update company information" service, and the company name availability checker if you plan to incorporate. Companies House applies "same as" rules (for example, it treats punctuation and words like "UK" or "services" as insignificant), so "Bright Spark Electrical Ltd" may be blocked by "Bright Spark Electrical Services Limited".

A match here does not automatically rule a name out, especially if the other business is in a completely different industry in a different county, but it is a yellow flag.

Step 2: Search the trademark registers

A business trading in Ireland can be affected by Irish national trademarks and EU trademarks, which cover all EU member states. A business trading in Northern Ireland or Great Britain is affected by UK trademarks. Search all that apply:

  • Intellectual Property Office of Ireland (IPOI): its online trade mark search covers Irish national marks.
  • UK Intellectual Property Office (UKIPO): the "Search for a trade mark" service covers UK marks, including the "comparable" UK marks created from EU marks after Brexit.
  • EUIPO / TMview: TMview searches the EU register plus many national offices in one go, and is the quickest way to get a wide view.
  • WIPO Global Brand Database: useful if you plan to sell internationally, as it includes international registrations under the Madrid system.

Understanding classes

Trademarks are registered for specific goods and services, grouped into 45 "Nice classes". A mark for "Willow" in class 30 (bakery goods) does not necessarily stop you using "Willow" for a plumbing business in class 37. Common classes for small businesses include:

When you search, look for marks that are identical or similar in sound, look or meaning, in your classes and related ones. "Glo Beauty" and "Glow Beauty" are likely to be treated as similar; so might "Kavora" and "Cavora".

Step 3: Check the real world

Registers only show part of the picture. Spend twenty minutes on:

  • Google, with the name in quotes plus your industry and location.
  • Google Maps, for local businesses that never registered anything.
  • Instagram, Facebook and TikTok, where many small brands live.
  • Etsy, Amazon and Not On The High Street if you sell products.
  • Domain lookups, because an active website on the .com or .ie suggests someone is using the name.

Step 4: Read the results sensibly

Here is a rough way to sort what you find:

  • Green: no identical or similar marks in your classes; no businesses using it in your sector or area. Proceed, and consider registering your own trademark.
  • Amber: a similar name in a different sector, or a dead trademark (expired or withdrawn), or a small business far away. Usually workable, but think about whether you could grow into conflict.
  • Red: an identical or very similar live trademark in your class, or an established competitor nearby using the name. Choose another name. It is not worth the risk.

Step 5: Consider registering your own trademark

If the name passes, protecting it is usually cheaper than people expect. You can file directly with the IPOI, the UKIPO or the EUIPO online. At the time of writing an EU trade mark costs €850 for one class filed online and a UK application starts at around £170 online for one class; Irish national fees are on the IPOI website. An EU mark covers Ireland and every other EU country; if you also sell into Northern Ireland or Great Britain, you will need a UK mark too.

Some practical tips:

  • Register the word mark (the name in plain text) first; it gives broader protection than a logo.
  • Only claim the classes you genuinely use or plan to use soon.
  • You can use ™ with any name you treat as a trademark, but only use ® once it is registered; falsely claiming registration can be an offence.
  • For a business you are investing heavily in, a professional clearance search from a trade mark attorney is good value. They catch similar marks that simple searches miss.

A quick checklist

  1. CRO (CORE) and/or Companies House search.
  2. IPOI, UKIPO and TMview searches in your classes.
  3. Google, Maps and social media searches.
  4. Domain and handle checks (our generator's "Check domain" button helps here).
  5. Register the business or company name; consider a trademark.

Done properly, these checks give you the confidence to invest in signs, packaging and marketing knowing the name is yours to build on.
