--- title: How to Test a Business Name Before You Launch short: Testing a business name description: Simple, low-cost ways to test a business name with real customers before you commit: the phone test, five-second test, survey questions, search checks and a scoring sheet. date: 2026-11-16 ---

Choosing a business name usually ends with a gut decision, and gut decisions are fine, as long as they are informed. Spending an afternoon testing your shortlist with real people catches problems you cannot see because you are too close to the name. You do not need a research budget. Everything in this guide can be done for free with a phone, a few friends-of-friends and a spreadsheet.

Step 1: Get down to a shortlist of three to five

Testing works best with a small number of genuinely different options. If your shortlist has ten names that are all variations of "Bright Spark Electrical", you will not learn much. Aim for a mix: perhaps one descriptive name, one local name and one brandable name. Our business name generator can help you find options in each style.

Before testing anything, do the quick availability checks: domain, social handles, company register and trademark search. There is no point testing a name you cannot use. Our trademark guide covers the registers for Ireland and the UK.

Step 2: The five-minute solo tests

Do these yourself first. They filter out weak names without bothering anyone.

  • The phone test: answer an imaginary call: "Good morning, ______, how can I help?" Does it roll off the tongue, or do you stumble?
  • The email test: write out the email address: info@______.ie. Is it easy to read out over the phone?
  • The run-together test: type the name with no spaces, all lowercase. Look for accidental words hiding inside it.
  • The search test: Google the name with and without your industry. Who else appears? Would a customer confuse you?
  • The ten-year test: imagine the business in ten years: bigger, maybe selling different things, maybe in a different town. Does the name still fit?

Step 3: Test with real potential customers

Friends and family are kind, which makes them poor testers. Try to find five to ten people who match your ideal customer: neighbours for a local trade, parents for a children's brand, other crafters for a craft shop. Local Facebook groups, colleagues and friends-of-friends are all fair game. Then run these tests:

The hearing test

Say each name out loud once, then ask the person to write it down. Count how many spell it correctly. If fewer than four in five get it right, you will lose people who hear about you by word of mouth, which for most small businesses is the main source of customers.

The five-second test

Show each name written down for five seconds, then ask: "What do you think this business does?" and "What would you expect to pay?" You are listening for whether the name points people in roughly the right direction. A premium beauty studio whose name makes people think "budget nail bar" has a problem.

The recall test

Show all your shortlisted names, chat about something else for ten minutes, then ask which names they remember. Memorable names win word-of-mouth.

The preference question (last)

Only at the end, ask which name they like best and why. Preference is the least useful signal on its own, because people often pick the most familiar-sounding option, but the "why" is gold. Write down their exact words; they often make great taglines.

Step 4: Try a quick online test

If you want more data, you can run cheap online tests:

  • Polls: an Instagram Story poll or a post in a relevant community. Keep it to two options at a time and remember your followers are not a neutral sample.
  • Survey tools: free tools like Google Forms let you repeat the five-second and recall tests at a larger scale.
  • Mock-ups: put each name onto a simple mock-up (a shopfront, a van, a product label or an app icon) and ask which business people would choose. Seeing names in context changes opinions more than you might expect.

Be careful about announcing a name publicly before you have secured the domain and handles, and avoid revealing names you have not yet checked.

Step 5: Score your shortlist

Pull the results into a simple scoring sheet. Give each name a score from 1 to 5 on each of these and add them up:

CriterionWhat a 5 looks like
SpellableAlmost everyone spells it correctly after hearing it once
MeaningfulPeople guess the industry or the feeling you want
MemorableMost people recall it after ten minutes
DistinctiveNo similar names in your sector or area
AvailableDomain, main social handles and registers are clear
Future-proofStill fits if you grow, move or add products
You love itYou would be proud to say it a thousand times

The highest score is not automatically the winner, but if your favourite scores much lower than another option, ask yourself honestly why you prefer it.

Step 6: Decide, secure and move on

Once you have decided, act quickly: register the domain and social handles the same day, register the business name with the CRO (Ireland) if you are not trading under your own name, or form your company, and consider a trademark if you are investing in the brand. Then stop second-guessing. Every successful business name felt slightly strange at first; it is the business you build around it that makes it feel right.

Quick recap

  1. Shortlist three to five genuinely different, available names.
  2. Run the solo tests: phone, email, run-together, search, ten-year.
  3. Test with five to ten real potential customers: hearing, five-second, recall, preference.
  4. Optionally, run a quick online poll or mock-up test.
  5. Score each name, decide, and secure everything the same day.
