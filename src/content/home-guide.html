How to choose a business name (that still works in five years)

A generator is brilliant for getting unstuck, but the final decision is yours. The best small-business names usually come from a short process: brainstorm widely, shortlist ruthlessly, then check that the name is actually free to use. Here is the process we recommend, whether you are opening a bakery in Galway, setting up as a plumber in Dublin or launching an Etsy shop from your kitchen table.

1. Start with what you want people to feel

Before you type a single keyword, write down three words that describe how you want customers to feel when they deal with you. A plumber might pick reliable, quick, local. A kawaii sticker shop might pick cute, cheerful, collectable. A consultant might pick calm, sharp, trustworthy. These words steer everything: they tell you whether you need a sensible descriptive name like "Liffey Plumbing & Heating" or something bouncy like "Pickle & Pop". Feed some of them into the generator as keywords and you will see the results shift.

2. Decide how descriptive you want to be

Every business name sits somewhere on a scale from descriptive (says exactly what you do, e.g. "Cork Cake Company") to abstract (an invented or borrowed word, e.g. "Kavora"). Descriptive names are easier to understand and often help with local search, but they are harder to trademark and can box you in if you expand. Abstract names are easier to protect and to find a domain for, but you will have to work harder to explain what you do. Many of the strongest small-business names sit in the middle: a distinctive word plus a plain descriptor, such as "Solas Photography" or "Copper Kettle Café". Use the Style buttons above to explore each end of the scale.

3. Keep it short, sayable and spellable

Say each shortlisted name out loud as if you were answering the phone, then imagine a customer trying to spell it into Google after hearing it once. This "radio test" weeds out a surprising number of clever names. Aim for two or three syllables for brandable names, avoid unusual spellings (swapping "c" for "k" or dropping vowels), and be careful with hyphens and numbers, because people forget them. If you love an Irish-language word, check the pronunciation and spelling are friendly to your audience; a fada is lovely on a sign but disappears in a web address.

4. Think about growth

"Ballymun Bike Repairs" is a clear name until you open a second shop in Swords or start selling e-scooters. Ask yourself where the business might be in five years. Place names are excellent for local trust, so if you use one, consider whether it should be the main part of the name or a tagline you can change later. The same goes for very specific products: "The Cupcake Corner" is harder to stretch to wedding cakes and catering than "The Little Oven".

5. Check it is actually available

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that causes expensive rebrands. For each name on your shortlist, check:

  • Company and business name registers: in Ireland, search the CRO register on CORE; in the UK, use the Companies House search. In Ireland you must register a business name with the CRO (form RBN1) if you trade under anything other than your own name.
  • Trademarks: search the Intellectual Property Office of Ireland (IPOI), the UK IPO and the EUIPO (TMview covers many registers at once). Our guide to checking trademarks in Ireland and the UK walks through each one.
  • Domains: use the "Check domain" button on any result to look up the .com, .ie and .co.uk. A matching domain is not essential, but it helps enormously.
  • Social handles: the same button opens Instagram, TikTok, Facebook and X so you can see whether the handle is taken.
  • Google: search the name plus your town and your industry. If a similar business already ranks for it, keep looking.

6. Test it on real people

Share your top three names with five or six people who match your ideal customers, not just friends and family. Ask them what kind of business each name suggests and what price they would expect to pay. If people guess your industry and pricing roughly right, the name is doing its job. If they think your premium beauty studio sounds like a discount chain, that is useful to know before you print 500 business cards.
