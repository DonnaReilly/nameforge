12 Business Naming Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

By the NameForge team · Updated 2026-11-17 · 4 min read

Most naming mistakes are not obvious at the time. The name sounds great in your head, your friends like it, and the logo looks lovely. The problems surface later: customers cannot find you online, a competitor sends a solicitor's letter, or you outgrow a name that only made sense for your first product. Here are the twelve mistakes we see most often, and how to avoid each one.

Mistakes that make you hard to find

1. A name nobody can spell

Creative spellings ("Kreative Kutz", "Phresh") look fun on a sign but fail the moment someone hears your name and tries to type it. Every misspelling is a lost visit. Fix: do the "phone test": say the name to someone and ask them to write it down. If more than one in five gets it wrong, change it, or make sure you own the common misspellings as domains.

2. Too generic to stand out

"Quality Cleaning Services" and "Best Builders" describe you, but they also describe thousands of other businesses. You will struggle to rank in search, cannot trademark it, and customers will not remember which "quality cleaners" they used last time. Fix: add a distinctive element: a local landmark, an unusual word, an Irish word with a meaning you like, or your own name.

3. Forgetting about the domain and social handles

Discovering after the launch that your exact name is a busy American company's .com and every social handle is taken means years of confusion. Fix: check domains and handles at the shortlist stage. The "Check domain" button on our generator opens them all at once.

4. Names that are too long

"The Little Handmade Kawaii Sticker & Stationery Company" will not fit on a business card, an Instagram bio or an Etsy shop name (which is limited to 20 characters). Fix: keep the core name to one to three words and move the description into a tagline.

Mistakes that limit your growth

5. Boxing yourself in geographically

"Ballincollig Bakes" is charming until you open a second shop in Douglas or start shipping nationwide. Fix: if you want local flavour, use a regional nickname, a river or a feature that travels well, or keep the location in your tagline instead of the name.

6. Boxing yourself in by product

The same applies to products: "Phone Case Palace" becomes awkward the day you add laptop sleeves. Fix: name the category or the feeling rather than one product, e.g. "The Little Oven" rather than "Cupcake Corner".

7. Following a trend that dates quickly

Dropping vowels ("Bxtr"), adding "-ly" or "-ify" to everything, or naming yourself after a viral meme can feel fresh now and dated in three years. Fix: trends are fine if the underlying name is still easy to say and spell. Ask yourself whether it will sound sensible on an invoice in ten years.

Mistakes that cause legal headaches

8. Skipping the trademark check

This is the expensive one. If another business has a registered trademark for a similar name in your industry, they can force you to stop, and you lose the signage, packaging and goodwill you have built. Fix: search the IPOI, UKIPO and EUIPO registers (TMview covers many at once) as well as the CRO and Companies House. Our trademark checking guide shows you how.

9. Assuming the CRO registration protects you

In Ireland, registering a business name with the CRO is a legal requirement if you trade under anything other than your own name, but it does not give you exclusive rights. Fix: treat the CRO registration as admin, and a trademark as protection.

10. Using words you are not entitled to use

Some words imply status or regulation: "Bank", "Insurance", "Society", "Council", "Royal", "Chartered", "Registered" and so on. Company registries may refuse them or require permission, and some professions are legally protected. Fix: check the registry guidance before using any word that suggests official approval or a regulated profession.

Mistakes in the process itself

11. Asking the wrong people

Your mum and your best friend love you; they will probably love the name too. Their opinion matters less than your customers'. Fix: show your top three names to five or six people who fit your target market and ask what kind of business each one suggests and what they would expect to pay. Their guesses tell you if the name sends the right signal.

12. Falling for the first idea, or never deciding at all

Some people commit to their first idea without checking anything; others spend six months agonising and never launch. Fix: give yourself a deadline. Spend an hour generating 100+ ideas, shortlist ten, check five, test three, pick one. A good name you launch with beats a perfect name you never use.

A simple naming process that avoids all twelve

  1. Brainstorm widely: use the name generator with different styles and keywords; aim for 100+ ideas.
  2. Shortlist ten: remove anything hard to spell, too long or too generic.
  3. Check five: domain, social handles, CRO/Companies House and trademarks.
  4. Test three: on real potential customers, out loud and written down.
  5. Decide and register: secure the domain and handles the same day, register the business name, and consider a trademark.

It is not glamorous, but it is the difference between a name that quietly works for you for decades and one that costs you a rebrand.
